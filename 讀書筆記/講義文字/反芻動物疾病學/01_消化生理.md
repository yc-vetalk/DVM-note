## p.12

Ruminants
Ruminants (order Artiodactyla, sub-order Ruminantia) are 
even-toed, herbivorous grazing or browsing mammals. They 
have the ability to regurgitate ingested food back into their 
mouths and chew it again before sending it to a specialized 
stomach (such as the rumen). By microbial fermentation, 
they are able to extract nutrients from plant-based foods. 
Because this process occurs in the front part of the digestive 
system, it is known as foregut fermentation.

## p.27

Essential Nutrients—Water
A lactating dairy cow requires 4–5 L of water per kg of DMI, 
and an additional 3–5 L of water for every kg of milk produced. A 
non-lactating cow (with a DMI of 10 kg/day) drinks approximately 
40 L of water daily. However, when producing 16–20 kg of milk per 
day, it requires an additional 70 L of water; for a cow yielding 35 
kg/d, total water consumption can approach 100 L.

## p.50

Rumen degradable protein (RDP)
Rumen microbes play a critical role in the digestion and 
utilization of dietary protein. After the ingestion of dietary crude 
protein (CP, a collective term for nitrogenous substances), 
approximately 60–65% or more is degraded by microbes into 
ammonia. This ammonia serves as the required nitrogen source for 
microbial proliferation and is subsequently synthesized into 
bacterial protein or amino acids.

## p.51

Rumen degradable protein (RDP)
This bacterial protein passes into the small intestine, where it is 
digested and utilized by the cow. According to the 
recommendations by NASEM (2021) (formerly NRC, the National 
Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine), for a dairy cow 
yielding 30 kg/d, RDP should account for about 60–65% of the 
dietary CP, while RUP should account for approximately 35–40% of 
the CP.

Dry Period
cows are dried off, a stage that refers to the cessation 
of lactation. The ideal dry period lasts 45–60 days and 
encompasses three critical objectives: (1) mammary gland 
repair and regeneration, (2) meeting the demands of rapid 
fetal development, and (3) colostrum synthesis. Additionally, 
this period allows cows to manage their body condition, 
accumulating body fat to cope with the postpartum negative 
energy balance.

Hypocalcemia / milk fever
Hypocalcemia, or milk fever, is a common metabolic disorder in dairy cows 
characterized by low blood calcium levels. It presents in both clinical and 
subclinical forms, severely impacting cow health during their most vulnerable 
stage: the transition period. Immediately after calving, dairy cows require a 
massive amount of Ca. They initially draw this Ca from the bloodstream, and 
subsequently mobilize it from their diet and bone reserves. If unable to mobilize 
sufficient Ca, affected cows may become unable to move or stand normally and 
will experience a decreased appetite, ultimately leading to compromised overall 
health and poor lactation performance.

## p.61

Dietary cation-anion difference (DCAD) 
DCAD is a specialized dietary metric for ruminants, typically 
expressed in milliequivalents (mEq). 
The calculation formula is: (Na+ + K+) - (Cl- + S-). 
Feeding a negative DCAD diet mildly acidifies the blood, 
stimulating the cow to mobilize stored Ca from her bones. This acts 
as an internal regulatory mechanism for Ca homeostasis, preparing 
the cow to cope with the massive loss of Ca associated with 
lactation.
