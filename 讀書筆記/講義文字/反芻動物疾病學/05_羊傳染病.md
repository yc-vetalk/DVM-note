## p.3

Malignant Catarrhal Fever (MCF)
• MCF is a frequently fatal, lymphoproliferative disease 
caused by a groups of viruses that belong to the family 
Herpesviridae, subfamily Gammaherpesvirinae, genus 
Macavirus.
• MCF affects mainly ruminants and, rarely, swine.
• European report of sheep-associated MCF in 1798
• In 1850, the Maasai people in Africa discovered that cattle 
in close contact with wildebeests are prone to MCF.

## p.4

Etiology
• Two major groups of MCF viruses: 
✓Alcelaphine herpesvirus-1 (AlHV1), the wildebeest-
associated MCF virus; Alcelaphine MCF occur in most 
African countries.
✓Ovine herpesvirus-2 (OvHV2), the sheep-associated 
MCF virus; Sheep-Associated MCF occur worldwide; 
causes MCF in cervids
• Other MCF viruses:
✓Caprine herpesvirus 2 (CpHV-2), the caprine-associated 
MCF virus; endemic in most domesticated goat 
populations worldwide and causes MCF in cervids.

## p.7

Epidemiology
• Reservoir hosts (sheep, goats, wildebeest) 
• Clinically susceptible (dead-end) hosts (cattle, bison, deer), 
which do not shed virus
• The morbidity rate varies; usually sporadic and presents as 
a single or small number of cases.
• The disease with both agents is almost always fatal in cattle 
and is an important disease of farmed deer.

## p.8

Transmission
• AlHV1 
Transmission in wildebeest occurs mainly perinatally, but 
some calves may become infected in utero; all calves 
become infected within the first few months of life and 
remain carriers for life.
• OvHV2:
A proportion of lambs are infected in utero with most 
lambs becoming infected when 3–6 months old by aerosol 
transmission
• Both AlHV1 and OvHV2 transmitted to susceptible host by 
contact or aerosol, primarily from respiratory secretions of 
wildebeest calves (AlHV1) and weaned lambs (OvHV2) 
under 1 year of age.

## p.10

Clinical Presentation
• Incubation period in natural infection varies from 3 to 8 weeks
• Different forms of presentation that are actually gradations
✓Peracute
✓Alimentary tract form
✓Common “head-and-eye” form 
✓Mild form
• In cattle, lymphadenopathy and severe eye lesions are more frequent, and 
hemorrhagic enteritis and cystitis less frequent, than in deer and bison. 
• The presence of nasal discharge, corneal edema, fever, and 
lymphadenopathy is very helpful in differentiating MCF from mucosal 
disease or bluetongue.
→All of these forms may be produced 
during infection with one strain.

## p.18

Introduction
• An arthropod-borne, noncontagious viral disease primarily of 
domestic and wild ruminants (eg. white-tailed deer).
• Cause by bluetongue virus (BTV), an Orbivirus in the family 
Reoviridae with a genome composed of 10 dsRNA segments.
→ several (at least 29) serotypes and considerable genetic 
heterogeneity.
• The occurrence of different BTV serotypes varies by 
geographic region.
→ the occurrence and activity of the Culicoides vectors
• WOAH notifiable disease.
Culicoides biting midges庫蠓(糠蚊)

Epidemiology
• Cattle are the reservoir and amplification hosts.

## p.33

Introduction
• Highly contagious disease produced by sheeppox (SPPV) 
and goatpox (GTPV) viruses (both are of the genus 
Capripoxvirus), collectively called capripox infections.
• WOAH notifiable disease

wool, skin condemnation, and loss of exports

Caprine Arthritis 
Encephalitis (CAE)

## p.44

Introduction
• A persistent lentiviral infection of goats
• Multiple clinical presentations: 
✓Leukoencephalomyelitis, affecting young kids
✓Chronic, hyperplastic polysynovitis
✓Indurative mastitis
✓Interstitial pneumonia.
• WOAH notifiable disease
• First described in 1974. CAE is now an important infectious 
disease of goats worldwide. In some herds, the infection rate is 
as high as 80%.
• Taiwan first confirmed the presence of CAE through pathology 
and EM in 1993.

Genus Lentivirus, Orthoretrovirinae, family of Retroviridae
It is often grouped together with Maedi-visna (MV) of sheeps, 
also known as ovine progressive pneumonia (OPP), as the 
small ruminant lentiviruses (SRLVs)

## p.47

Transmission
• All breeds and all ages are susceptible to infection; 
differences, if occur, likely reflect differences in management
• Ingestion of infected colostrum or milk. 
• The presence of antibody in colostrum does not prevent 
infection.
→ Neonates should be removed from the doe immediately 
after birth and reared on pasteurized milk, and many can be 
reared free from infection
• Contact transmission will result in the spread of the disease.
• In utero (rare)

## p.48

Pathogenesis
• Animals infected at birth remain persistently infected for 
life, although only a proportion, typically from 10% to 30%, 
will develop clinical disease.
• The virus infects a variety of cell types including choroid 
plexus, mammary epithelium, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, 
and monocytes; however, replication primarily takes place 
in mature macrophages; the virus is activated when 
monocytes mature into macrophages and is persistent in 
macrophages, microglia, monocytes, and dendritic cells
• Lesions (lymphoproliferative) have been shown to be at 
least partially immune-mediated as well as due to direct 
destructive effect of virus

## p.49

Clinical Presentation
• Most infected goats are asymptomatic (~70%)
• Neurologic syndrome (2-4 months old): 
✓Neurologic signs are due to motor spinal dysfunction without signs 
of cerebral disease 
✓Clinical signs are progressive: Caudal ataxia and trembling of the 
lips → Afebrile; progressive posterior ataxia and extensor paralysis 
and tetraplegia →death from starvation (wasting) or secondary 
infection 
✓Once paralytic signs develop, death is certain 
• Arthritis: Lameness, joint effusion, enlarged carpal joints, high 
prevalence of carpal hygromas 
• Interstitial pneumonia: Dyspnea and coughing, cachexia 
• Mastitis: Udder firm, swollen; reduced milk production

Etiology
• Coxiella burnetii, a gram-negative, obligate intracellular 
bacterium belonging to the class Gammaproteobacteria. 
• Two morphologically distinct forms: 
 -- Large cell variant: pleomorphic, metabolically active 
(replicating) and sensitive to environmental conditions
 -- Small cell variant: short-rod-shaped, nonreplicating, 
resistant to environmental stressors 
• Two antigenic types: 
 -- Type I: pathogenic in animals and the wild. 
 -- Type II is produced by serial passages in chick embryo 
yolk sacs and cell cultures; less pathogenic.

## p.66

Transmission
• Worldwide distribution
• Inhalation of aerosols or dust containing the pathogen is 
the main route of transmission. Other routes include skin 
wounds, conjunctival contact, and the digestive tract.
• The pathogen is present in the animal's mammary glands, 
mammary lymph nodes, uterus, and placenta. 
• It is transmitted through blood, milk, feces, urine, amniotic 
fluid, and secretions. 
• Ticks can also be a vector (rare).

## p.69

Clinical Presentation
• Incubation period: varies greatly, from about 9 to 28 days. 
• Most ruminants are subclinically infected. Possible symptoms 
include mild fever and various reproductive disorders including 
infertility, abortion (孕後期), stillbirths, weak offspring, retained 
placenta, and endometritis.
• In humans: the most common symptoms are fever, fatigue, 
headache, altered consciousness, nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea. 
It can lead to fatal "acute respiratory distress syndrome" due to 
atypical pneumonia. Other conditions include granulomatous 
hepatitis, hepatomegaly, and jaundice. 
Chronic infection can also cause meningitis, orchitis, myocarditis, 
endocarditis, and nephritis

## p.70

Pathological Findings
• Gross Pathology: In animals, lesions are limited to the 
placenta, which is thickened, leathery, and has multifocal to 
coalescing areas of calcification.
• Microscopic lesions: In animals, necrotizing 
intercotyledonary placentitis with intracellular organisms 
+/- vasculitis. In humans, there are granulomatous nodules 
in various affected tissues

## p.76

Enzootic Abortion of Ewes (EAE)
• Zoonosis / WOAH notifiable disease
• Similar disease presentation in goats
• Also cause abortion in cattle, pigs, and horses but the 
incidence is quite low.

## p.77

Etiology
• Chlamydophila abortus, is a small, gram-negative, obligate 
intracytoplasmic bacterium. 
• Two forms: 
 -- Elementary body (EB): a small, dense sphere that can 
remain infectious for several months in low-temperature 
environments. 
-- Reticulate body (RB: non-infectious
• Resistant to acids, alkalis, toluene-based compounds (甲苯
基化合物), and lime (石灰), but sensitive to most 
disinfectants, such as 1% sodium hypochlorite, 70% ethanol, 
glutaraldehyde, and formaldehyde; also inactivated by heat.
77
Up to several months if the 
temperature is low or close to 0 °C.

## p.78

Transmission
• Introduction of latently infected ewe
• The main source of transmission is the aborted placentas or 
uterine secretions from sick ewes.
• The placenta and uterine/vaginal secretions of sick ewes 
contain a large amount of C. abortus. 
• Infected ewes shed the organism for a week before aborting 
and 2 weeks afterward.
• Common routes include oral or nasal inoculation, vertical 
transmission, and the presence of C. abortus in the feces 
and urine of cattle and sheep, as well as in goat milk.

## p.79

Epidemiology
• Worldwide distributed except for Australia and New Zealand
• In Taiwan: the seropositive rate of goats collected from six farms during 
2007–2011 was 60% (18/30). Aborted fetuses checked for the presence 
of six abortigenic pathogens by PCR revealed highest positive rate for Cp. 
abortus of 26.1% (6/23). → Major causative agent for goat abortion.
• The main hosts are sheep and goats, but it can also infect cattle, deer, 
llamas, rabbits, and horses. 
• In a newly infected flock, over 30% of pregnant ewes abort in the last 3 
weeks of gestation or give premature birth to weak or dead lambs.

## p.80

Pathogenesis
• Following infection, it is thought that the organism 
resides first in the tonsil and is then disseminated 
by blood and lymph to other organs, although the 
site of latent infection is not definitely known.
• Release from the latent state during pregnancy is 
thought to be caused by immune modulation and 
leads to bacteremia and infection of the placenta.

## p.81

Clinical Presentation
⚫Abortion, stillbirths, or the birth of weak lambs in the last 2-
3 weeks of pregnancy.
⚫Pregnant ewes often lack overt clinical signs. 
⚫In some cases, a small amount of reddish or brownish 
vaginal discharge may be seen before the abortion. 
Retained placenta and mastitis are not common.
⚫After the abortion, the ewe may recover quickly, but some 
may continue to have a dark brown vaginal discharge.

## p.82

Pathological Findings
• Aborted fetuses: 
-- Typically no gross abnormalities
-- Histologically, there may be mononuclear cell infiltration 
of hepatic portal areas and areas of hepatitis.
• Placental cotyledons are necrotic and hemorrhagic, and the 
intercotyledonary areas are thickened, edematous, and 
leathery. Vasculitis are more common than infection with C. 
burnetiid.

## p.85

Diagnosis
C. abortus should be considered when ewes have abortions, 
stillbirths, or weak lambs in late pregnancy. 
• Laboratory Diagnosis: 
-- Tissue smear : smear from the sick ewe's placenta and 
uterine secretions and examine for C. abortus EBs after 
Giemsa or acid-fast stains.
-- C. abortus EBs cannot be differentiated from C. burnetii. 
• ELISA, IFA, PCR or pathogen isolation

## p.87

Prevention & Control
• Separating C. abortus-infected ewes from their lambs and 
treating them with long-acting oxytetracycline is the main 
way to control the persistent infection in the flock. 
• Vaccination (either live or inactivated works) can reduce 
abortions in ewes but cannot prevent C. abortus infection in 
lambs.
• The zoonotic potential warrants caution when handling 
potentially infected material, and specimens should be 
properly packaged when submitted.
