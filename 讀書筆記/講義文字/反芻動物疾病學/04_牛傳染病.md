## p.4

Bovine Salmonellosis
• Numerous serotypes of Salmonella spp. have been isolated from 
cattle, and many of them are considered infectious in cattle.
• Most important: S Typhimurium & S Dublin (both are zoonotic)
• S Typhimurium can cause diseases in a variety of animals, including 
humans, cattle, sheep, and pigs
• S Dublin is cattle-adapted and has a greater tendency to cause 
invasive/systemic disease..
• Usually endemic, sometime causes major outbreak.
• Multidrug-resistance are common in US & Canada isolates

## p.8

Pathogenesis & Immunity
• GI tract → mesenteric LN → systemic dissemination
• S Dublin has adapted to evade host immune response
• Virulence factors: Salmonella Pathogenicity Islands (SPI), 
toxins, flagella (motility), fimbriae (adhesion)…
• S Dublin encodes the Type III Secretion System from SPI-1 
and SPI-2: invade the intestine and spread to systemic sites, 
respectively
• Host immunity is crucial in the outcome of clinical 
salmonellosis
-- Young calves with an immature immune system → high 
morbidity and high mortality
8
Vaccination and colostrum are important!

## p.9

Clinical Presentation
• More severe in S Dublin comparing to other Salmonella 
serovar
• Vary, depending on age, herd condition…
• All ages are susceptible but most common in calves at 2–12 
weeks of age
• In naive herds, the pathogen is rapidly transmitted, and an 
outbreak ensues. 
• Although most Salmonella infections present as GI disease 
(so diarrhea is common), S Dublin infection most often 
presents as respiratory illness.

## p.19

Diagnosis
• Detection of the organism by culture or PCR
-- Feces (relatively low sensitivity in culture)
-- Specimens from necropsy (recommended for culture)
-- Blood in cases of suspected bacteremia
• Necropsy – No pathognomonic lesions
-- Common: pulmonary congestion, suppurative pneumonia, 
catarrhal hemorrhagic enteritis, mesenteric lymphadenitis; 
and hepatosplenomegaly… → septicemia
• ELISA – monitoring 
-- Serum and milk

## p.20

Prevention & Control
Goal : Minimize pathogen exposure & maximize pathogen 
resistance
Strategy
• Farm management
• Sanitation
--Disinfectants can be used: halogens (eg, dilute chlorine 
bleach), phenols, quaternary ammonium compounds, and 
oxidizing agents (eg, potassium peroxymonosulfate).
• Biosecurity
-- Rodent control
• Vaccination: live, attenuated whole-bacterium vaccines
Both humeral & cell-mediated immunity are important!

## p.22

Bovine Cryptosporidiosis
• Worldwide distributed
• Many species and genotypes of Cryptosporidium. C. parvum 
(formerly C parvum type II) is zoonotic and infective to many animals, 
including people and calves. C. hominis (formerly C parvum type I) 
has a narrow host range and is transmitted primarily from person-to-
person.
• Other cryptosporidial species have been isolated from cattle: C 
andersoni, C bovis, & C ryanae
• C parvum is a common cause of calf diarrhea, and its oocysts have 
been detected in the feces of 70% of 1- to 3-week-old dairy calves

Bovine Viral Diarrhea

## p.39

Etiology
• Bovine viral diarrhea virus (BVDV)
• Three distinct species:
--BVDV-1 (Pestivirus bovis), 
--BVDV-2 (Pestivirus tauri), 
--HoBi-like virus or BVDV-3 (Pestivirus brazilense)
• Genus Pestivirus, family Flaviviridae
• Two biotypes: cytopathic (CP) & non-cytopathic (NCP)
 ** The biotype does not reflect the ability to cause disease
• Wide host range: cattle, sheep, swine, goats, and other wild 
animals
• Immunosuppression

• Widespread in cattle population due to persistently infected 
(PI) animals associated with prenatal infection (during the 
first 4 months of fetal development) with NCP virus strains.
• The prevalence of infection vary depending on the

• Economic significance of BVDV is related to reproductive

## p.41

Transmission
• The major source of infection is the PI viremic animals.
• The estimated mean animal prevalence PI in a herd is about 
1%–2%.
• BVDV is present in all secretion and excretion.
• Route of transmission: 
✓ Direct contact: nose-to-nose
✓ Transplacental 
✓ Indirect contact: airborne, flies, fomites…
✓ Artificial insemination/embryo transfer
✓ Contaminated vaccination (from FCS used in TC)

## p.42

Pathogenesis & Immunity
• The virus infects cells of the innate immune system 
affecting the function of neutrophils, monocytes, 
macrophages, and dendritic cells.
→ Immunosuppression
→ BVDV may also participate in the development of BRD
• Glycoprotein E2 is the major antigenic target for antibodies
• Cattle respond to natural infections or vaccination with MLV 
vaccines exhibit a long-lasting immunity
• High levels of VN Ab prevent disease following homologous 
challenge but animals may still develop viremia and shed 
virus in nasal secretion.

## p.46

Classical/Acute BVD
• Usually associated with NCP strains
• Transient infection in immunocompetent cattle over 6m/o
• Clinical signs are mostly inapparent to subclinical; if present, 
include fever, mild oculonasal discharge, tachypnea, 
diarrhea…
• Transient leukopenia → secondary infection
• High morbidity, low mortality
• Lifelong immunity

## p.47

Severe Acute BVD
• High morbidity, high (25%) mortality
• All animals at all ages are susceptible
• Usually due to NCP BVDV-2
• Clinically indistinguishable from mucosal disease (MD) and 
signs may include: high fever (up to 42 °C), respiratory 
distress, anorexia, profound watery diarrhea & agalactia
• Thrombocytopenic/hemorrhagic syndrome
• PI calves are commonly born several months following the 
outbreaks.

Predisposing to Pneumonia
• BHV-1 virus impairs the function of alveolar macrophages,

principle virulence factor) to bronchoalveolar leukocytes, 
and increases their killing.
The net effect is that BHV-1 infection amplifies the 
detrimental effect of M. haemolytica in the lung

## p.110

Rinderpest (cattle plaque)

## p.111

• Caused by rinderpest virus belonging to the genus morbillivirus 
• Declared globally eradicated in 2011 by OIE/WOAH.
• Transmission required direct or close indirect contact
• Often occurred as epizootics associated with a very high 
morbidity & mortality rate due to severe diarrhea/dysentery and 
dehydration.
• The virus infect mucosal epithelial cells, monocyte and 
lymphocytes → mucosal erosion/ulceration & lymphoid necrosis
• Clinical signs include fever, anorexia, depression, and oculonasal 
discharges developed, followed by necrotic lesions on the gums, 
buccal mucosa, and tongue.
• Affected animals develop immunosuppression and are prone to 
2nd bacterial infection
• Recovery may take weeks.

## p.112

Bovine Tuberculosis

## p.113

Bovine Tuberculosis (Bovine TB)
• Primarily by M. bovis, but also by M. caprae and to a lesser 
extent M. tuberculosis.
-- Member of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) 
-- Acid-fast, slow-growing intracellular bacterium 
• Chronic granulomatous disease 
• Broad host range
-- Cattle are the major domestic reservoir 
-- Wildlife reservoirs are important in some countries 
• Zoonotic 
• WOAH-listed disease

## p.114

Transmission
• Among cattle:
-- Primarily respiratory route 
-- Less commonly ingestion
• In other species:
-- Aerosol/direct contact 
-- consumption of contaminated 
 unpasteurized milk

## p.120

Diagnosis & Control
Ante-mortem diagnosis 
• Tuberculin skin test 
-- Measures delayed-type hypersensitivity (Type IV) 
-- Intradermal injection of PPD 
-- Read at approximately 72 h 
➢Single intradermal tuberculin test (SITT) 
-- bovine PPD 
➢Comparative intradermal tuberculin test (CITT)
-- bovine PPD + avian PPD → comparison helps distinguish 
sensitization to MTBC from environmental/nontuberculous 
mycobacteria 
• IFN-γ release assay 
-- Measures cell-mediated immune response in blood 
-- Can complement skin testing
