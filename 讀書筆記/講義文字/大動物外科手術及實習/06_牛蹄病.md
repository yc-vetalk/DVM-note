## p.4

Biomechanics of the cow shows that the front feet bear weight in a more flexible manner than the hind feet. 
Also the forefeet support heavier weights than the hind feet. When studying the movements of the hind feet, 
a lot of weight appears to be shifting from one foot to the other, even when the cow is standing still. A 
weighing test with scales proves that this is the case. It would not be a problem if the shifting weight was 
spread evenly on both claws of the foot. The truth is that the outer claw takes more weight than the inner 
claw.
If we compare the size and the weight-bearing surface, the outer claw is larger and its weight-bearing 
surface is flatter. The sole of the inner claw slopes at a sharper angle and provides little stability, especially 
on a hard surface. The outer claw must compensate for this by taking more weight. Consequently, it will 
wear thin sooner than the inner claw. As a reaction the corium (living tissue) produces extra horn which, in 
the long run, will result in the outer claw being overburdened.

## p.5

Inside the foot the last bone, the pedal bone, takes and transfers the weight of the cow to the corium and 
then to the sole. Between the pedal bone and the corium a digital cushion functions as a shock absorber. 
Normally the shock is absorbed sufficiently, but when soles are very thick the cushion cannot absorb the 
shock properly. The pedal bone hits the corium often and hard so that capillaries burst and a bruising (blood 
stain) shows in the sole. If this goes on the corium will get so badly damaged that it no longer forms horn on 
that spot and in the sole a hole appears, called a sole ulcer. Sole ulcers are also called typical sole ulcers. 
The part of the pedal bone causing this ulcer shows a small lump at that spot. This causes the (typical) ulcer. 
By trimming back the sole of the outer claw to its normal thickness sole ulcers can be prevented.

The most common causes of lameness in cattle are: 
foul in the foot, white line disease, sole ulceration, penetration of the hoof by sharp objects, heel erosion, 
digital dermatitis and laminitis.
 Swelling of the skin around the distal limb
 Foul presents as a swelling of skin between heels and accessory digits caused by inflammation of 
 the skin and subcutaneous tissue. The claws may be forced apart with necrotic interdigital skin. 
 Infection of the synovial structures of the distal limb, including the distal and proximal 
 interphalangeal joints, results in severe generalised swelling of the distal limb.

Examination of the sole
Laminitis 
This causes pressure pain and loss of‘adhesion’of the horn to the underlying structures of the foot. The blood 
supply to the structures supporting and producing the horn of the hoof is compromised, and poor quality soft horn 
may result. Laminitis may predispose the foot to sole ulceration, white line disease, rotation and penetration of the 
sole by P3. Adouble sole may follow a bout of laminitis. Laminitis presents with a yellow exudative/ haemorrhagic 
discolouration of the solar horn.

## p.15

Examination of the sole
 Classical solar ulcers 
These are recognised as circumscribed areas where horn is missing or the horn is of very poor quality. The 
corium is exposed or has been protected by the production of granulation tissue. The ulcers occur towards the 
back of the sole, two-thirds of the distance between the toe and the heel directly over the posterior border of 
the pedal bone. They are usually the size of a bottle top. Less commonly solar heel ulcers may be present 
which are set further back on the sole. Ulcers are often bloody due to presence of granulation tissue, and very 
painful.

## p.16

Examination of the sole
 White line disease 
The white line is a point of weakness, and dirt may become impacted and provide a portal of entry for 
infection if the integrity of the join between the horn of the wall and the horn of the sole is compromised. 
Once the claw has been trimmed affected regions of the white line can be readily identified as distinct black 
thick zones.
 Solar abscess 
White line disease can result in ascending infections or lateral infections. The latter produce solar 
abscesses. These may be detected during corrective trimming by a sudden thinning of the sole followed by 
the appearance and drainage of pus. Alternatively, it may present as a painful pliable thin sole at the end of 
Stage 1b of the foot trimming process. Pain testers can be useful to localize the area of pain, and further 
thinning of the sole may puncture the abscess. Occasionally, on applying pressure, pus can be seen to 
exude from the original portal of entry in the white line.
